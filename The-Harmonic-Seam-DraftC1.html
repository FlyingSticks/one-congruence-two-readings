Front matter — the footing this essay rests on

The claim of this essay is not a new geometry. It is a design principle, a drawing method, a formal system that turns out to underwrite both, and — the part the record now settles — a matter of priority and completion rather than invention. Three facts govern the citation posture, and I state them early.

1. The canon, 1979. In Graphics for Architecture (Forseth, with Vaughan; Wiley, 1979), I set down the constructive account of the existing families — orthographic plan, elevation and section; the paraline/axonometric obliques; perspective; and cast shadows — all built from descriptive geometry: fold lines, auxiliary views, cutting planes, measuring lines. That book is the evidence of standing: the work of someone who knew the whole projective canon from the inside, as constructions.

2. The fourth projection, 1984 — now read to its last plate. In Glide Projection: Lateral Architectural Drawing (Forseth; Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1984), I constructed the projection the canon lacked — glide — a complete, internally consistent measured system, and I distinguished it in the same book from its optically real twin, which I named double-slit projection and set aside because its receding lines curve (pp. 9–10). I derived glide's structure synthetically, stated its consistency conditions as a draftsman states them — no gaps or overlaps within or between lateral armatures, and a common infinity plane (p. 98) — recognized it was mechanizable, and deliberately left its analytic transcription undone, saying so on the page (p. 48). The applications chapter (pp. 103–122) named glide's native subjects — plan, site plan, section, views in low spatial relief (p. 103) — and staged, plate against plate, the comparison between glide and perspective on the same subjects, ending with an explicit invitation to the reader to compare. The book poses the question this essay answers. It never states the rule.

3. The independent rediscovery, 2002–2015. Working analytically from optics, computer vision arrived at the projection glide abstracts — the crossed-slit or X-slit camera (Pajdla 2002; Zomet, Feldman, Peleg & Weinshall 2003), placed in a continuous family of linear cameras by Yu & McMillan (2004) — and wrote the closed-form depth law (Yang, Lin, Kang & Yu 2015). None of it knew the architectural system existed. And the anticipation runs deeper than the projection: the 1984 book specifies that its freeform glides are observed part by part, in a continuous gliding motion, with local believability everywhere and no single position for the whole (p. 100) — the viewing protocol of the multiperspective mosaic, stated as a drawing instruction nineteen years before the mosaic was computed (Zomet et al. 2003; Román et al. 2004). It is also the origin of the name.

So the seam is exact, and it is the essay's spine: the seeing was synthetic and mine, in 1979–84; the calculation was analytic and theirs, two decades later; the machine that runs both is the present tense that reunites them. But there is a second seam beneath the first, and it is the one this combined draft exists to expose. The perspective I taught in 1979, the glide I built in 1984, and the cross-slit camera the vision literature computed all share one invariant — the harmonic cross-ratio — and it sits in each of them at a precisely locatable place: on the horizon line of a two-point perspective, at the neutral surface of the cross-slit rig, and in the depth law of a glide. That shared invariant is what lets the three projections be read as settings of one instrument rather than as three conventions. I do not claim the algebra. I claim the construction that contains it, the recognition that it was computable, the design principle it yields, and the harmonic thread that runs through all three.

I. The box

A cross-slit projection is an empirical fact. It can be demonstrated with a cardboard box, the same way a pinhole camera can: cut, point, expose. The pinhole camera forms its image by passing every projector ray through a single point. The cross-slit camera forms its image by passing every ray through two orthogonal slits — in the rig used throughout this work, a vertical slit in one plane and a horizontal slit in another, with the image plane behind both. It is possible to make cross-slit photographs with nothing more than a cross-slit arrangement of cardboard planes.

In mathematics the pinhole is reduced to a point, and the reduction hides a question. No physical pinhole is a point — light could not get through, and it would make no sense for every ray to emerge unscathed from a collision with every other ray. Any working pinhole has a diameter, which invites the question the magnifying glass makes vivid: how does an image invert at the focus? (A model of what happens to an image as it passes through the hole: demo 6.)

The cross-slit case carries the same disclaimer. Real slits must have width, else no light. That material fact cannot be ignored, but it can be separated from the underlying geometry and set aside — the way an architecture curriculum separates structures from strength of materials. Everything that follows makes that separation. The physics has done its job the moment the box produces a picture; from here the subject is the math and geometry of cross-slit projection, and the drawing system that abstracts it.

A word on names, since four circulate for one object. I called the optical projection double-slit in 1984; the vision literature says crossed-slits, X-slit, or cross-slit. The body of this essay says cross-slit for the optical projection and glide for its drawable idealization; double-slit appears only when citing the 1984 book or the geometry document that bears the name.

II. The drafting table

Cross-slit projection can be interpreted as an internally consistent system of projective geometry, akin to perspective projection. Both live comfortably inside Euclidean space: object, picture plane, and the ambient world coexist as points, lines, and planes on an x-y-z grid, and both project coherent images of three-dimensional objects onto a plane. The classical two-point perspective construction — plan above, elevation beside, sight rays through the station point, projectors dropped to the image area — nests several kinds of space in one drawing: a 3D Euclidean world, a picture plane inside it, and inside that, the projected image.

The cross-slit construction is only slightly different. Replace the station point with two slit lines seen edge-on: in the top view the vertical slit appears as a point; in the side view the horizontal slit does. Rays are traced through each in its own view, exactly as in the perspective construction, and the image is assembled on the picture plane. Structure can be imposed on the field itself with a Cartesian grid, so that whole rooms and objects in plan and elevation are cross-slit projected by drafting technique alone (demo 11, demo 13). This is the office method of the 1984 book — the image of a point found as the intersection of two planes, each through the point and one governing line (Glide Projection, pp. 45–59) — performed with the governing lines at two different depths.

One difference announces itself immediately at the drafting table: the depth lines bow. In one-point perspective the lines running into depth are straight spokes through the vanishing point. In the cross-slit image they curve — hyperbolic arcs sagging toward the central vanishing point, with asymptotes parallel to the coordinate grid. The straightness of straight lines is the first casualty of removing the focal point, and it is not a defect; it is the signature. (Formally this is the Bowing Theorem, Theorem 3 of the geometry document, with its three cases explored in demo 28.)

It was exactly this bow that, in 1984, made me set the optical projection aside. At a drafting table a curve you cannot construct with T-square and triangle is unbuildable and unteachable. So I kept the lateral, two-vanishing character of the true optical projection and refused its curvature — a straight-line idealization I called glide, bought at the price of confining itself to the shallow scenes where the bow would anyway be negligible. Glide and cross-slit are one congruence of lines read two ways: the cross-slit reads it as rays collapsed to their pierce-points on the picture plane; glide reads it as edges, each vertex drawn as a graduated projector with depth laid off as a linear graduation from footprint to vanishing point. The two readings fuse on the degenerate members of the family — the pinhole, the pushbrooms — and split exactly where the two governing lines are genuinely at two depths. Which reading one uses is the subject of the budget in section IV. Why both readings share one invariant is the subject of section V.

III. Reading depth

The bowing hints at something the pinhole cannot do: a single cross-slit image carries depth information in the shapes of things. A sphere, projected through a pinhole, remains a circle at any distance. Projected through crossed slits, it stretches into an ellipse, and the extent of the stretch is a function of depth. Programs written against the rig geometry can read depth of field directly from the ellipticity of known spheres scattered through the visual field (demo 14, demo 17).

This is the seed of the codec. If aspect ratio encodes depth, then a flat image of round targets is a volumetric recording, and decoding is geometry rather than inference (demo 18, demo 19, demo 20; overview in the codec readme). In the calibrated rig — screen at z = 0, vertical slit at Zv = 150, horizontal slit at Zh = 250 — the depth-from-aspect-ratio map turns out to be an exact Möbius transformation, which is a fact worth pausing on, because it means the codec's working formula was a piece of projective geometry before anyone recognized it as one. The recognition came later, and it reorganized everything. On provenance, to be exact: in the project's record the formula was written from the rig geometry first, as the codec's working law, and only afterwards recognized as Möbius and identified with the depth-dependent-aspect-ratio law Yang et al. had published in 2015. The priority of publication is theirs and is cited as such; the derivation from the rig is the project's own, and the two were found to agree.

The 1984 book had already equipped glide with this reading twice over, by construction. Because the governing lines sit at two different depths, the horizontal and vertical scales fall off at different rates; I split perspective's single "unity convergence" into an independent lateral convergence within the surface and a depth convergence into the picture, and proved the two independent by the continuous-vantage-plane argument (pp. 43–44). That two-axis structure is the whole engine: its ratio — the proportion a square casts — is a function of depth, so a glide drawing is a measured drawing. The midpoint method develops a diminishing scale of true depths from the diagonals of a rectangle (pp. 75–76); the scaled glide lets depth be read directly with a ruler along an orthogonal field line, up to a bounding transverse plane that guards the infinity plane, beyond which the diminishing scale resumes (pp. 100–101). The reference vanishing frame, whose sight line bisects the image into two equal parts, fixes the depth law as a constructed proportion — the 1:2 ratio, stated as a ratio (pp. 48–50). The closed-form transcription of that proportion is precisely what I left undone and what the vision literature later supplied (Yang et al. 2015). And the transcription, when it came, was a cross-ratio.

IV. The bargain and the budget

Every architectural drawing strikes a bargain with the third dimension, and every convention we have inherited strikes it differently. The plan and the section keep measure and surrender the view; the axonometric keeps measure and parallelism and surrenders the way a room meets the eye; perspective keeps the eye and surrenders measure — and, the part we tend not to say aloud, surrenders it unevenly, so that a wide façade forced into a single perspective splays and bloats at its edges while a deep nave sits perfectly.

The principle is a budget. A projection has a fixed quantity of fidelity to spend, and it must spend it on one axis at the expense of another. Perspective spends lavishly on depth: its cue for distance is diminishing size, a signal of unbounded range, which is why it renders a corridor receding to infinity without complaint — but it pays on breadth, where a wide field forces the picture plane to meet the cone of rays obliquely and the margins stretch, a distortion that is radial, position-dependent, and removable by no single correction. The complementary projection makes the opposite bargain: it spends on breadth, laying wide scenes down gently, and pays on depth, where its distance cue saturates and — in its raw optical form — its receding lines bow.

State the two bargains exactly. In perspective the magnification of a feature is inversely proportional to its distance, a factor running the whole interval from unity to zero as the scene recedes; the range is unbounded, so depth resolution is effectively unlimited and a feature's proportions are preserved at every distance — a square stays a square. That last fact is the reason perspective cannot be measured for depth: shape carries no distance information. Glide, and the cross-slit projection it idealizes, invert every term. The gift is that proportion now encodes distance. The cost is the mirror of perspective's, and I stated its mechanism in the same book under "eccentric projective effects" (pp. 68–69): only one family of parallels can stay straight, two of the three axes must lie in transverse planes, and the third — depth — must be orthogonal for straight lines to survive; curvature, twisting, and stretching all grow with depth of field and vanish as the field goes shallow. This is why glide's distance cue is bounded and its native subject shallow. Bounded with a number: the aspect ratio a frontal square casts is AR(z) = (Zv/Zh)·(z − Zh)/(z − Zv), which tends to Zv/Zh = 0.6 as the scene recedes, so over the whole field beyond the far slit the cue runs only from 0 to 0.6 and flattens — where perspective's diminishing size runs the open interval down to zero and never flattens. The budget verdict for a scene of breadth B and depth extent Δ, drawn at slit separation D, is the comparison of Δ·D against B2: the product of depth and separation is what the anisotropy residual grows with, the square of the breadth is what perspective's marginal splay grows with, and the scene belongs to whichever projection its proportions make cheaper. The derivation and the constants are in A13; the plates of section VIII carry the comparison in their captions.

From this the design rule falls out with the force of arithmetic. Match the projection's benign axis to the scene's large axis. Deep and narrow — a nave, a light-well, a corridor, a stair section — belongs to perspective, whose unbounded depth cue has room to work and whose breadth penalty never engages. Broad and shallow — a façade, a wide wall section, a site of massing blocks, a floor plan read as a horizontal cut — belongs to glide, whose gentle breadth and correctable anisotropy keep the whole width legible and whose shallow depth is all such a scene asks. The plan, which is nothing but a shallow section through a building, is glide's most natural application of all.

It is here that the essay adds what the record does not yet hold. The optical projection and its analytic depth law belong to computer vision; the canon and its unification belong to the classical literature and to the General Linear Camera. The subject class already belongs to 1984. What the book does not do — what no one has done — is state the principle the plates enact: that these two projections partition the space of architectural subjects between them; that the partition is governed by the opposition of a bounded depth cue to an unbounded one; that the designer should choose deliberately by a scene's proportions; and that one of the two yields a measured drawing for the broad, shallow subjects perspective serves worst. In 1984 I chose the subjects correctly and exhibited the choice; I did not state the rule that made it correct. priced design principle, first statement claimed — a priority claim, not a theorem, falsifiable by a single prior source that states the rule. The sources checked, and the one still to be read, are listed in the appendix. Any earlier statement of the partition, in either literature, retires this tag.

V. The harmonic thread

The budget of section IV treats perspective and glide as opposite bargains. The remarkable thing is that the two bargains are struck with the same coin. Perspective and cross-slit projection — and glide, which idealizes the latter — share one deep invariant: the harmonic cross-ratio. This section follows that invariant from the horizon line of a perspective to the neutral surface of the rig, and shows that the parameter which opens one projection into the other carries the invariant with it.

The harmonic in perspective

In a classical two-point perspective the harmonic cross-ratio appears on the horizon line as two pairs of vanishing points arranged harmonically. Take the vanishing points VL and VR of the two horizontal edge directions of a rectangular building, and the vanishing points V45 and V135 of its two plan diagonals. Because the diagonal directions bisect the angle between the edge directions at the station point, and the internal and external bisectors of an angle form a harmonic pencil with its arms, the four points sit in harmonic position: (VL, VR; V45, V135) = −1, for any station point and any orientation of the building. Every draftsman who has laid out a two-point perspective with a 45° measuring point has used this fact without naming it. The harmonic value is the one cross-ratio that the perspective apparatus manufactures for free.

The harmonic in the cross-slit

In the cross-slit rig the same value appears in terms of the slits themselves. Each slit magnifies its own axis by the perspective factor for its own depth: the vertical slit scales x by Zv/(Zv − z), the horizontal scales y by Zh/(Zh − z). Their ratio — the aspect ratio a frontal square casts at depth z — is

AR(z) = (Zv/Zh) · (z − Zh)/(z − Zv),

a Möbius map of depth. The depth at which the two slits' magnifications exactly cancel — equal in size, opposite in sign — is the harmonic mean of the slit depths,

z* = 2 Zv Zh / (Zv + Zh) = 187.5 in the rig,

and there the aspect ratio passes through −1 exactly. This is the neutral surface: the locus where the four canonical points of a ray sit in harmonic position, cross-ratio −1, and where the rig itself sits at the harmonic value on both slits at once. Perspective manufactures −1 across its horizon; the cross-slit manufactures −1 at a depth. The same invariant, in one case a direction, in the other a distance.

The dial carries the harmonic with it

Now take the perspective station point at depth Zp and split it — not metaphorically but by construction — into two lines at two depths, letting a single parameter D open the gap: Zv = Zp − D/2, Zh = Zp + D/2. At D = 0 the lines coincide and the drawing is an ordinary one-point perspective; open D and it becomes a cross-slit, or — read as edges rather than rays — a glide. The full account of this dial is section VI. What belongs here is where the harmonic locus goes as the dial turns. Substituting, the neutral surface sits at

z* = Zp − D2/(4 Zp).

At D = 0 the neutral surface coincides with the station point: the harmonic locus of the cross-slit collapses into the eye, which is exactly where perspective keeps it — the harmonic pencil described above lives at the station point and is only read off on the horizon. As D opens, the neutral surface moves forward toward the picture plane, but only to second order in D. Near the pinhole the harmonic locus barely stirs; the two projections part company slowly at first and only then decisively. (In the rig, Zp = 200 and D = 100 give 200 − 12.5 = 187.5, as above.) priced theorem — an algebraic identity, checked at the rig constants.

There is a companion observation worth recording. Splitting the station point into two depths yields not one center but three, ordered by the classical inequality of means: the arithmetic mean Zp = 200 is the old station point; the harmonic mean z* = 187.5 is the neutral surface; and between them the geometric mean √(ZvZh) ≈ 193.6 is the radius of the mirror circle in the depth section — the anchor sphere under which, in the six-step duality check, the cross-slit congruence proved self-dual and the exchange between glide and cross-slit readings became a per-channel inversion z ↦ ZvZh/z. All three collapse to Zp at D = 0. Perspective has one center; the cross-slit has a center for each of the three means, and the harmonic one is where the picture squares itself. priced the means are theorem-grade; the identification of the geometric mean with the self-duality anchor is verified numerically and pending symbolic confirmation.

Glide's place in the thread

Glide is not a bystander to this. The cross-slit and the glide are one congruence of lines read two ways, and the two readings graduate depth by reciprocal gauges: where the cross-slit scales a channel by 1/(1 + rζ), the glide scales it by (1 + rζ), and the product is 1 along every ray. priced theorem — the gauge product is an identity of the two readings, verified in the inversion viewer.

The mirror relation VM·VI = VP2

On the picture-plane diagonal, with both slits at one depth, the glide mark M and the cross-slit image I of the same scene point sit with the vanishing point V and the up-projection P of the slit crossing in the relation VM·VI = VP2 — M and I are inverse points in the mirror circle of radius VP, which is to say harmonic conjugates with respect to the two points where that circle cuts the diagonal. Read as graduations, the cross-slit's is the Möbius one, with the plane at infinity landing at a finite mark, and the glide's is the equal-step one, with infinity left off the end; the two are conjugate, and the conjugating map is the inversion z ↦ ZvZh/z about the geometric mean of the slit depths — the same anchor the three-means paragraph named. So the harmonic value enters glide not as a depth it marks but as the relation it holds to its optical original: a glide is the cross-slit reflected through the mirror of the neutral geometry, and the neutral surface is where the reflection is the identity. priced VM·VI = VP2 is verified in the assembly instrument on the seam; the conjugacy of the two graduations through the geometric-mean inversion is verified numerically in the six-step check and the two-graduations ledger, pending a symbolic pass.

The gauge is unity at the neutral surface

The correction of section VII reads the aspect ratio a known square casts at the working depth — the gauge — and multiplies the compressed axis by its reciprocal. At the neutral surface the gauge is −1: the frontal square is already square in magnitude, mirrored on one axis, and the reciprocal factor is unity. The harmonic locus is thus the one depth at which the budget's anisotropy costs nothing. It is a landmark, not a working plane: it lies between the slits, where the anisotropy changes fastest with depth, so the clean band for a drawing is centered elsewhere (A13.3). But it fixes the meaning of the correction. Squaring the gauge at any working depth z0 is the act of carrying the neutral surface's harmonic balance to z0 by one global factor. priced theorem for the gauge value; the band statement is A13's.

Six numbers, not one

The harmonic case is the tip of a larger structure. A cross-ratio λ of four collinear points is not one number but six, depending on the ordering: λ, 1/λ, 1−λ, 1/(1−λ), λ/(λ−1), (λ−1)/λ — the orbit of λ under the permutation group. Generically all six are distinct (the anharmonic case). At the harmonic value the six merge to three: {−1, 2, ½}. At the equianharmonic value they merge further to two. This collapse — what it looks like, what it means, where it lives — is worked through in the Overflow studies (the ceiling of the tower, the equianharmonic collapse, the modular corner).

An early note in this project reads: cross-slit projection doesn't fit into this framework. That turned out to be exactly half right. It does not fit because the framework assumes a single number, and the fundamental invariant of cross-slit geometry is a pair: the bi-cross-ratio (λ, μ), one cross-ratio per slit, equivalently one cross-ratio valued in the double numbers. The six-element orbit acts diagonally on the pair, which doubles the classical taxonomy into configurations with no pinhole analogue: harmonic on one slit and generic on the other (split-harmonic), harmonic on both (bi-harmonic, (−1, −1)), harmonic on one and equianharmonic on the other. The collapse from six to three now happens per axis, and the neutral surface is its physical address — the one depth where the rig sits at (−1, −1). The framework did not exclude cross-slit projection; it was one factor of it.

This is also, I now think, the algebraic content of the 1984 independence argument. When I split perspective's single convergence into a lateral convergence and a depth convergence and proved them independent by the continuous-vantage-plane argument (pp. 43–44), I was establishing, as a draftsman establishes things, that the two governing lines carry two separate cross-ratios — that a glide's depth law is a pair and not a number. The vision literature wrote the pair as a ratio of two Möbius factors; the chain geometry of section X writes it as a single double-number cross-ratio. The drawing had the pair first. priced observation — a correspondence between the 1984 proof and the bi-cross-ratio, not yet a theorem mapping one onto the other.

VI. The instrument with a dial

A reader who has followed the budget will want to see the two projections as one adjustable thing rather than two rival diagrams, and they are. The dial of section V, stated fully: split the station point into two lines at Zv = Zp − D/2 and Zh = Zp + D/2. At D = 0 the two convergences fuse into one and the drawing is a one-point perspective. Open D and the convergences separate: the image acquires its two independent vanishing behaviors, the aspect ratio of a frontal square begins to encode depth, the neutral surface detaches from the eye and slides forward by D2/4Zp, and the drawing becomes a glide. Push D wider and the depth cue sharpens while the clean band narrows, exactly as the budget identity requires. One knob runs the whole family, pinhole at one end, wide glide at the other, and the office method of the 1984 book is simply this construction performed at a chosen D.

That the family is continuous, and that these two projections are neighbors on it rather than strangers, is the content the vision literature supplied independently: Yu and McMillan's General Linear Camera places pinhole, orthographic, pushbroom, and two-slit cameras in one space of ray-planes, with the crossed-slit — glide's optical original — the immediate term past perspective. The analytic home of the dial is theirs; the dial itself, as a drawing operation an architect performs with a governing line and a straightedge, is the 1984 construction.

The demonstration is where the reader stops reading and starts turning the knob. The interactive companion carries D as a live control: drag it from zero and watch a scene pass from perspective through glide, the vanishing structure splitting in real time, the frontal squares stretching as the aspect-ratio cue engages, the neutral surface — drawn as a chip — leaving the station point. What was a static plate in 1984 becomes a single continuous morph, the comparison the book staged now animated along its missing parameter. The boundary conditions are the book's own: slits coincident gives perspective; all receding orthogonals parallel gives the paraline, which by definition has no infinity plane (p. 101); between those limits lies the glide, with its infinity plane and its measured recession.

VII. The correction

Glide's cost is a per-depth anisotropy: at any single working depth the frontal plane maps by a pure affine stretch of one axis against the other, identical at every position across the field. That last clause is the whole of why the cost is payable. A distortion that is the same everywhere on the sheet is undone everywhere by the same operation — and the operation is a single reciprocal scale.

Concretely: read the gauge AR(z0) at the working depth from a known square. Multiply the compressed axis by 1/AR(z0). The sheet is now square at z0, exactly, at every field position — verified to machine precision in A13.3, and implemented in the interactive as the ▢ button that squares the gauge with one factor. This is anamorphic correction in the classical sense the book already indexes (p. 31), now given its coefficient: not an artist's trial-and-error unsquaring but a computed reciprocal read straight off the depth — and, in the reading of section V, the neutral surface's harmonic balance transported to the working plane.

Two honest boundaries frame the operation. First, it is exact only on the reference plane; through a scene of depth extent Δ the residual anisotropy after squaring is εx = D·Δ / (2(z0 − Zv)(z0 − Zh)) to first order (A13.3), growing linearly with the depth of field and vanishing as the field goes shallow. At the rig constants and a 1% tolerance this buys a clean band of about four units per percent — a twenty-unit band at 5% — beyond which one reintroduces the diminishing scale the way the scaled glide reintroduces it past its bounding transverse plane (pp. 100–101). Second, the correction addresses the first-order anisotropy, not the second-order bow of receding lines; but the bow is O(Δ2) (A13.6), subordinate to the anisotropy inside any working band for which the squaring was worth doing. The correction is therefore a ruler's worth of work — read the gauge, apply one factor — where perspective's marginal error, radial and position-dependent, admits no global repair at all.

VIII. Plates

The plates do one job: show the rule deciding, correctly, on real architectural subjects, and once show it deciding against a subject chosen to earn the wrong answer. Each is a scene rendered through the dial at the D its proportions call for, with the gauge factor applied, and each carries its Δ·D against B2 in the caption so the reader sees the budget arithmetic land.

The wide section. A broad, shallow building section — many bays across, little depth into the sheet — is glide's home ground. Here B is large and Δ small, so Δ·D ≪ B2 by a wide margin; the whole width stays legible where a single perspective would splay its end bays, and the squared gauge returns true story heights across the span. The 1984 vanishing-axis and double-herringbone glide sections (pp. 111, 113) are the hand-drawn ancestors; the plate is the same subject with the number attached.

The floor plan with its wall-depth reveal. A plan is a shallow section through a building, maximal in breadth and minimal in depth — glide's most natural subject by arithmetic, since the ratio Δ·D/B2 is smallest of all. The reveal is the payoff the orthographic plan cannot give: because proportion encodes depth, wall thickness and the drop into a window jamb read directly off the sheet as measured recession, not as a separate section. This is the plate that earns the word measured.

The site plan in relief. Massing blocks on a broad ground plane, low spatial relief — the book's own stated domain (p. 103). The relief reads as glide depth; the correction squares the ground grid; the breadth that would bow a wide perspective lies down gently under glide's nearer-slit convergence.

The courtyard. An interior looked into from above — the aerial one-point courtyard of the 1984 applications chapter (pp. 108–109), now rendered as glide so that the four walls' depth and the floor's true proportions coexist on one measured sheet.

The gauge through the dial. New in this draft. A frontal square at the working depth z0 = 350, drawn through the dial at D = 0, 50, 100. At D = 0 it is a square, because the drawing is a perspective. As D opens it stretches — AR = 1, 0.556, 0.300 — and one reciprocal factor re-squares it at each setting. The plan strip below each panel shows the neutral surface leaving the station point by D2/4Zp = 0, 3.1, 12.5. The plate shows the harmonic locus doing the correction's bookkeeping while the correction itself is a ruler's worth of work.

The deliberately wrong case. Force a deep, narrow subject — a corridor or nave receding far into the sheet — into glide. Now Δ is large and B small, so Δ·D ≫ B2; the budget verdict is perspective, and glide pays its full price. The plate shows it honestly: the aspect-ratio cue saturates toward Zv/Zh and stops resolving depth, the bow the correction cannot reach grows with the depth of field, and the residual εx runs past any tolerance the single gauge factor can meet. The caption reports the number that predicted the failure before the drawing was made. Showing the budget's cost is not a concession; it is the proof that the rule is a rule and not a preference.

IX. Doubling the rig

One page of the early notes proposes a way to read depth rather than compute it: take two cross-slit projections of the same scene at once, rotated 90° to each other about the viewing axis, separated by filters — red and blue, or polarized — and read the disparity not as positional offset, as in ordinary stereo, but as relative shape distortion. The greater the separation between related pairs of red and blue features, the closer the feature.

That sketch became the dual cross-slit rig. Worked out formally, the second projection is a second tetrahedron rotated 90° about the shared axis; the combined aperture at each slit plane is a cross; and the channel-to-arm assignment solves image registration by construction. The zero-disparity surface — the shape-horopter, where the two channels fuse — is the neutral surface again, z* = 187.5: the harmonic locus of section V, now doing optical work. The dual ratio cancels ellipticity errors that fool a single channel, exactly and algebraically, and two independent channels buy the expected √2 in signal-to-noise. The idea that began as a colored-filter sketch closes the loop with the oldest invariant in the project. The harmonic value that the draftsman reads off a horizon line, the geometer locates at a depth, and the drawing squares its gauge against is the same value the doubled camera fuses on.

X. Euclid revisited, and the seam

The original ambition for the formal side of this work was a synthetic geometry — primitives, axioms, theorems — based on Euclidean geometry with a rewritten fifth postulate. Euclid's five are worth reciting because the fifth is the one under pressure. Straight lines join any two points; segments extend; circles exist with any center and radius; right angles are congruent; and — the parallel postulate — through a point not on a line there passes exactly one line parallel to it. The fifth assumes a flat plane.

The cross-slit rig puts a different surface in front of you. The projector rays through the two slits rule a doubly-ruled surface — a hyperbolic paraboloid or hyperboloid, depending on section — and on such a surface the parallel question changes character. There is a central ray, perpendicular to every generator that crosses it, plausibly the shortest bridge between all the skew-parallel lines of the ruling. The suspicion recorded in the early notes was that somewhere in this surface the fifth postulate was being renegotiated.

The suspicion was right, but the renegotiation happened somewhere unexpected. The finished synthetic system (Double-Slit Geometry: Definitions, Axioms, and Theorems) is not Euclid patched; it is a chain geometry in the tradition of Walter Benz — a Minkowski plane, the split-signature sibling of the Möbius plane. And in that system the fate of the fifth postulate is legible. Parallelism does not weaken; it splits in two. Two rays are 1-parallel if they share a foot on the first slit, 2-parallel if they share a foot on the second, and these are separate equivalence relations that never confer. Through any ray there is a full pencil of each kind. The structural role Euclid V plays — pinning down exactly one privileged line through an outside point — is played by the Touching Axiom: through a point off a chain there is exactly one chain meeting it at a single prescribed ray. Euclid's parallel survives as tangency. And the 1984 independence of lateral and depth convergence (pp. 43–44) is, read in this light, the drafting-table form of the split: two parallelisms that never confer, drawn before they were axiomatized. priced observation, as in section V.

Where the point went. The pinhole's ray space is a projective plane P2; the cross-slit's is P1 × P1 — two projective lines interleaved, a grid rather than a plane, coordinatized not by ℝ or ℂ but by the double numbers ℝ ⊕ ℝ. The number system of the geometry is two copies of the real line glued along their arithmetic. Explore the axioms interactively in the geometry companion.

With cross-ratios, the point is the primitive: four points on a line, one number. What is the primitive of cross-slit geometry? Not the point. Two generic rays of the system meet nowhere — the only meetings allowed are between parallel rays, and those meetings are exiled to the slits themselves. The union of the two slits, belonging to every ray's addressing but to no ray's interior, is the seam. The seam between two systems is the atom, and the entire geometry is built out of it.

In this work the interface-belonging-to-neither is a locus arrived at, found after the fact as the excluded seam. That inversion may be why cross-slit has always felt like home ground rather than a result reached from elsewhere. What the seam is gets no assertion here — establishing that is precisely the project, and the companion essay The Seam is where the establishing happens: algebraically the seam is the zero-divisor locus of the double numbers, and since ℝ ⊕ ℝ is the split-complex plane of 1+1 relativity, two rays that meet in space are null-separated in the image. The seam is the picture's light cone. The ground for that sentence is short and belongs here. A double number is a pair (a, b) with componentwise arithmetic; its zero-divisors are the pairs with ab = 0, the two axes. In P1 × P1 a ray is addressed by its two feet, one on each slit, and two rays meet exactly when they share a foot — exactly when the difference of their addresses has a zero component, that is, when it is a zero-divisor. Meeting in space is therefore the null condition ab = 0 of the double-number form, and the seam, the set of null directions, is the light cone of that form. What The Seam establishes beyond this is what the cone does to the picture — that is the companion's work, not this paragraph's. priced the null condition ab = 0 for meeting rays is theorem-grade; the identification of ℝ ⊕ ℝ with the split-complex plane is classical; the sentence "the seam is the picture's light cone" is, in this essay, a rhyme with 1+1 relativity, and stays at that grade until the companion shows the cone doing optical work.

This non-concurrence isn't a weaker pinhole. It is the exact refusal of the one thing that makes a pinhole a pinhole.

And the refusal is nearly unique. Kummer's classical study of congruences of order one says that if every scene point is to be threaded by exactly one ray, the aperture has very few options: a point, two skew lines with their degenerations, or the secants of a twisted cubic. The camera built here is not one arbitrary alternative among many; it is nearly the only alternative geometry permits. Which is also why glide, the drawable idealization of that camera, is not one arbitrary drawing convention among many. The canon had four measured projections and one picture; geometry allowed one more, and 1984 built it.

XI. Construction and computation

The plates close the practical argument; a quieter one remains. This essay has completed a system I left open in 1984 — but the shape of that completion is itself the point, and it runs against a common assumption about how knowledge in a mathematical field is supposed to arrive.

The assumption is that one knows a geometry when one can compute it: that the formula is the knowledge and the drawing merely its illustration. The history of glide says otherwise. In 1984 I built the projection as a construction — governing lines, intersecting planes, a measured-depth apparatus, closure conditions stated as a draftsman states them — and I knew it was consistent, knew it was mechanizable, and said on the page that its effects could be verified with definitions and theorems I was giving without proving (p. 48). That is knowledge by construction: complete, correct, and deliberately stopped short of algebra.

The formula came two decades later, from a different discipline reasoning from optics, not knowing the drawing existed — the crossed-slit camera rediscovered (Pajdla 2002; Zomet et al. 2003), placed in its family (Yu & McMillan 2004), and finally written as a closed-form depth-from-aspect-ratio law (Yang et al. 2015). Their abstract opens on the very fact my construction turned on: that in perspective a frontal object's aspect ratio is invariant to depth, and that the crossed-slit breaks this invariance into a usable depth cue. They arrived analytically at what the drawing had guaranteed synthetically.

And the formal footing came last, from a third direction: the recognition that the depth law was a cross-ratio, that the rig's neutral surface was its harmonic value, that the camera's ray space was P1 × P1 over the double numbers, and that the whole assembly was a chain geometry in the tradition of Benz. Honesty about provenance: the underlying objects are classical. The family of transversals to two skew lines is the hyperbolic linear congruence, studied since Plücker, Kummer, and Sturm in the nineteenth century. The abstract axiomatics instantiates the theory of chain geometries and Minkowski planes developed by the school of Walter Benz. What this body of work contributes is the assembly: the Erlangen-style presentation of the camera as a geometry in its own right; the identification of the working codec formula as a cross-ratio, which explains both its Möbius form and its noise behavior; the drafting-table constructions of 1984 and their completion; the design principle they license; the harmonic thread that runs from the horizon line to the neutral surface to the gauge; the interactive demonstrations that verify every numbered theorem against the live rig; and the dual-rig development. Speculative material stays quarantined from the defensible claims, and the demos carry their own check scripts.

So the thing was known-by-construction in 1984 and known-by-formula in 2015, and only now, with the algebra and the machine joined, is it known-by-machine — a drawing you can dimension, compute, and turn on a dial. Three books trace the arc: Graphics for Architecture drew the projections the canon already had; Glide Projection constructed the one it lacked; the present work computes it. The seam between the second and the third is the seam between seeing and calculating, and it is exact — the seeing was mine and synthetic, the calculation theirs and analytic, and neither is diminished by the other.

Two closing witnesses, both from the 1984 book's last pages, seal the thesis rather than merely decorating it. The first is the name. The book instructs that its freeform glides be read not from a single station but part by part, in a continuous sweeping motion, with local believability everywhere and no one position commanding the whole (p. 100) — the exact viewing protocol of the multiperspective mosaic that computer vision would build nineteen years later, and the reason the drawing is called glide. The seeing anticipated not only the projection but the way it must be looked at. The second is the reach backward: the applications chapter closes on a Pompeiian wall mural reconstructed as a double-herringbone glide (p. 122), tying the fourth projection to the book's own history of pictorial space (Viola-Zanini 1629; Pozzo 1693; pp. 19–23). Antiquity's scenographers drew, by eye and intuition, what the fourth projection draws by rule — and the rule, now, computes. The geometer who builds a thing correctly can see that it is computable without being the one to compute it. That is the whole claim, and glide is its proof. The boxes were cardboard; the geometry, it turns out, was always chain.
