One Congruence, Two Readings · Chapter 1

The congruence

One set of lines — and the two ways of drawing it that the rest of the book keeps apart

There is one object in this book. Through a every scene point sends one ray to one image point, and all the rays share a centre. Replace the pinhole by two at two depths and every scene point still sends exactly one line — the unique line through the point that meets both slits — but the lines no longer share a centre. That two-parameter family is a , classical since Kummer, and it is the only thing any chapter draws. What changes from chapter to chapter is not the lines but the given to them: the same congruence used as edges under parallel light is the 1984 drawing construction, the ; used as rays over a rectilinear box it is the camera. The drafting table is where both are visible at once, and the point of this chapter is only to make the reader see one set of lines where two subjects have always been taught.

The congruence

Fix a picture plane at z = 0, a vertical slit at depth Zv and a horizontal slit at depth Zh. To each scene point X there corresponds one line ℓ(X): the transversal through X of the two slit lines. The family {ℓ} is a (1,1) congruence — one line through a general point, one line in a general plane — and its lines pierce the picture plane in the image.

Two graduations can be laid along ℓ(X). The glide marks depth in equal steps (depth lines straight, the picture plane true-size); the cross-slit marks it by foreshortening (each slit magnifies its own axis by the perspective factor for its own depth). Neither graduation changes the lines. The pinhole is the case Zv = Zh: the congruence collapses to a pencil and the two graduations coincide.

Not a theorem — a definition, and the standing convention for every figure that follows.

1.1 · Through a pinhole, through two slits

Before either reading is named, the congruence can be looked at as a light field. The base engine draws the lines themselves: choose a target depth, and every point of the frontal square at that depth is joined to the picture plane by its one transversal of the two slits. Slide the slits apart and the bundle stops being a cone; slide them together and it becomes one. The number in the corner — the aspect a frontal square casts — is the first hint that the lines carry depth information a pinhole does not, and Chapter 6 will cash it. For now it is enough to watch the ruled surface and believe that it is one thing.

A room under the same lines makes the second point. Its straight depth edges arrive on the screen as curves — each a Möbius graph, a hyperbola with axis-parallel asymptotes, the Bowing Theorem of the companions. That bow is the cross-slit reading's signature, and Chapter 3 will show it is proportional to one cubic. The glide reading of the same room, in demo 11, has no bow at all: same lines, straight tracks.

1.2 · The drafting table

Sheet 35 is the congruence laid out as a draftsman would lay it out: plan, side and front on one board, the two slits at the rig depths Zv = 150 and Zh = 250, a box between near and far, and the screen at zero. The trace runs one point of the box to its image by the two-view construction — down to the vertical slit in plan, across to the horizontal slit in side, and up to the front — and the sweep does it for every point on an edge. This is the instrument that later measures the seam; here it is only asked to show that the cross-slit image is built by the same straightedge moves as any descriptive-geometry projection, with two centres instead of one.

1.3 · The same board, read as glide

Sheet 35·G keeps the armature and changes the graduation. The depth between the front face and the shared back wall is divided into sixteen measured levels, and both readings are pinned to agree at level 0 (true size) and level 16. In between, the glide runs its depth lines straight and its levels in equal steps, while the cross-slit bows its lines and crowds its levels by foreshortening; the chip shows where the glide's equal marks fall in harmonic progression along the cross-slit's ray, with the depth plane at infinity landing on the flat segment through the horizontal vanishing points. Toggle between the two readings with the box fixed and the whole book is in front of you: one drawn skeleton, two rulers laid along it.

1.4 · The inversion

Are parallel rays capturing a converging shape, or converging rays capturing a rectilinear one? Both — and choosing is the organizing move of the whole family. A projection is a congruence given a role. The drawn is the shadow of the congruence and is shared by both readings; only the graduations differ. The inversion sheet puts the choice on a marker: drag it about the and the two pictures — glide on the right, cross-slit on the left — agree or split as the congruence degenerates or does not.

The sheet records the fact this book is named for, and its two levels. The drawn lines agree wherever the congruence degenerates: the whole diagonal (pencils), the r = 0 edges (a slit at infinity), and the corners. The full maps — lines, graduation and fibres together — agree only at the corners: perspective, orthographic, the two . Between those two levels is the whole drama: one congruence, worn two ways, drawing the same lines and measuring them differently. Chapter 2 says what a reading is; Chapter 3 measures the gap between the two levels and finds it is one cubic.
