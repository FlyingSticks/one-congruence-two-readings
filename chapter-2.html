One Congruence, Two Readings · Chapter 2

Law and gauge

What a reading is — a law plus a graduation — and why no line carries both graduations at once

Chapter 1 left two readings on one set of lines and said only that they graduate the lines differently. This chapter says what a is. Two things had been hiding under the one word: the — the shape of the image machinery, each channel contracting toward its seat at its own rate, straight depth lines, a rate pair, the vanishing structure — and the , the scale laid along those lines. A reading is a law together with a class of gauges, and a picks the representative. So there is no single object called the glide: glide names the law, and the gauge remains to be chosen. The cross-slit is the same kind of law with no choice left — optics weld law and gauge together. That one difference, free against welded, is the whole content of "one is a tool, the other is physics," and the theorem beneath it is small enough to state in a sentence.

Theorem 0

A scale on a straight line cannot both mark equal steps and assign infinity a finite mark. A scale with equal steps is affine, and an affine map of the line sends ∞ to ∞.

The gauge keeps equal steps and leaves infinity unmarked; the gauge is a Möbius re-scaling that marks infinity at a finite point and pays for it with harmonic crowding. These are the two graduations. Quantitatively: over equal steps 0, unit, 2·unit, the second difference of the graduation times the finite mark of ∞ is 2(1 + d)/(2 + d) ≥ 1, d = D/unit. Either failure can be driven to zero — never both.

Proved where stated; the witness inequality verified numerically in the Reach-and-Turn smoke suite, limit 2 at the Reach end.

2.1 · Reach and Turn

The two gauges are two registers on one line. Reach counts: equal steps mean equal amounts, its move is the slide, which keeps every difference and breaks every ratio, and its one involution is the , the fold about the midpoint of the unit — the only element of the anharmonic group an affine engine can see. Turn compares: equal steps mean equal ratios, its move is the scale, which keeps every ratio and breaks every difference, and its involution is the , inside exchanged with outside across the mirror circle. On a line Turn is a Möbius coordinate: ∞ is a marked point, so some finite point — the , the eye — must be sent to ∞ in its place, and equal steps in depth become the harmonic progression k/(k+1). Reach is the register of the count, the covector, the stack of level planes. Turn is the register of foreshortening, of perspective, of the cross-slit with both feet finite.

2.2 · Free and welded

The glide's third pin is the drafter's: a depth line may be counted (Reach) or foreshortened (Turn) at will, and straightness survives every re-gauging because the law is affine in the gauge whatever the gauge is in depth. The cross-slit's third pin is a slit foot, placed by optics: rays through two slits fix both the bowing of the lines and the crowding along them, and no chip can re-graduate what the rig has ruled. What the rate square sorts, then, is not free against welded — that is fixed by the reading wherever you stand. It sorts whether the two laws agree. On the seam they rule the same straight line, and turning the glide's free gauge to Turn welds the readings into one map. Off the seam the camera's line bows away, and no gauge can follow it, because the gauge question never arises once the laws already disagree.

2.3 · One line, three rulers

Take a single line of the congruence in section and lay three rulers along it. Each is a three- coordinate. The cross-slit's pins are the surface point S, where the line pierces the picture plane, and the two slit feet F₁ and F₂ — placed by optics, not chosen. The glide gauges share S and choose the rest: Reach pins ∞ at ∞ and marks equal steps; Turn pins ∞ at 1 and sends the station to ∞, so its steps crowd harmonically. The fourth point on the line is the probe P, the one scene point, and it has no image of its own — every point of the line pierces the picture plane at the same footprint, which is why the probe can slide without the image moving. Because all three rulers are three-pin coordinates on one line, the cross-ratio of any four depths reads the same on every ruler. That is the . What differs is where each ruler puts the : Reach sends it off the end; Turn puts it at 1 by fiat; the cross-slit puts it at 1 − Z₂/Z₁, wherever the slits happen to be, and the rig has no say.

The instrument records the fact that closes the chapter's circle. The two pushbroom limits are the two glide gauges. Send F₂ far out and the ∞-pin walks onto the plane at infinity: the cross-slit ticks straighten into equal steps and the diamond leaves through the end — Reach. Send F₁ far out and the 1-pin walks onto the plane at infinity: the diamond settles at 1, x(z) becomes z/(z + |Z₂|), and the surviving slit is the station — Turn. Fuse the slits and the cross-slit ruler collapses to the pinhole's. So the pairing F₁ ↔ plane at infinity, F₂ ↔ station is not a convention imposed on the rig; it is what the rig itself does in the limit. And once the surface sits at 0, exactly one element of S₃ besides the identity keeps it there, x ↦ x/(x−1), and it swaps the names of the two feet. That single switch is the whole of the pairing question, and Chapter 4 will find it is one of six.

2.4 · Calibration, and the two balances

The readings meet by calibration: pin them to agree at chosen depth planes. This is not the board's invention but glide practice's own staging, generalized — the staging rectangle of the 1984 plates is the common ground every comparison stands on, and sheet 35·G extends it across the two practices with level 0 (the front face, true size) and level 16 (the deepest constructed plane) as shared pins. Between the pins the gauges disagree in character, equal spacing against harmonic crowding. Beyond them the vanishing hierarchy opens, and it is strict: 16 < h pair < v pair. Under the harmonic gauge it sharpens to theorem: the depth plane at infinity maps to the flat segment h₂·A·h₁ on one vanishing axis, the limit of measured depth, while the v pair lies beyond even that — unreachable at any depth, directional only.

The split rectangle sorts the two kinds of truth this leaves. Its multiplicative balance, (ac)(bd) = (ad)(bc), holds for every position of the point: Turn-side truth, an invariant. Its additive balance, ad = bc, closes only on the diagonal: Reach-side truth, a calibration, Euclid I.43. Both practices contain both kinds, but their characteristic truths sort by side — the cross-slit's signature laws are invariants (the cross-ratio readout that never moves, the bowing no slider can repeal), the glide's signature truths are measurements — and the interface between readings is purely Reach-side: shared levels are diagonal closures, true exactly where the construction puts them. The cross-ratio is the balance that never moved. Whether that split-rectangle diagonal is the rate-square diagonal ru = rv is carried here as check-pending, as the source carries it; Chapter 3 states the seam on the rate square in its own terms.

Three calibrations were tried on the way to the board, and they are one family. Write Xg and Xcs for the two marks of one true measure x. The transpose calibration is the geometric mean, Xg·Xcs = x², so inversion through x exchanges the marks; under that inversion the mirrored calibration splits into a conjugate pair, x = AM(Xg, x²/Xcs) and x = HM(Xcs, x²/Xg). The geometric calibration is the exchanger between the other two, and the measured board completes the family: pin two levels and the gauge between them ranges over the Möbius maps, the equal-step and harmonic scales the two distinguished members. That is the arithmetic Chapter 3 finds waiting on the seam.
