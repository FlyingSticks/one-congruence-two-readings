One Congruence, Two Readings · Chapter 5

Duality

Transpose is duality, at the right level — and the seam is self-dual

The two readings have looked like transposes of each other since Chapter 1: edges under parallel light against rays over a box, a stack of level planes against a pencil through a slit, the count against the compare. Projective geometry has a name for that exchange — , points for planes, joins for meets — and the temptation is to say that the glide reading simply is the cross-slit reading dualized. This chapter says exactly how much of that is true. The naive version is false, for a reason worth having. The right version holds, at the level of depth pencils rather than lines, once the rig is given the one piece of furniture the straightedge cannot supply; and when it holds, the fused locus of Chapter 3 turns out to be the self-dual locus, with the same cubic for its equation. The verdict rests on a numerical check that was lost and is now recovered and re-runnable.

The amended verdict (8.5′d)

With the canonical — the sphere x² + y² + z² = Z₁Z₂, centred at the principal point, radius the geometric mean of the slit depths, under which the two slits are mutually polar — the cross-slit congruence is ; the induced along the depth axis is z† = Z₁Z₂/z; in geometric-mean calibration the duality acts on calibrated depth as (channel swap) ∘ Reciprocal, so it conjugates the glide reading into the cross-slit reading up to the single ρ; and the fused locus is the self-dual locus, equation Δ, on the interior and the diagonal.

Two amendments. The fixed element arrives composed with the apparatus channel swap, which is forced, not chosen: mutually polar slits exchange under δ. And the claim lives at depth-pencil level — the pointwise, per-line version is false, the per-line conjugacy being line-dependent.

5.1 · Why duality cannot see the split

Start with what duality is not able to do. Within bare projective geometry all perspectivities between two lines form one orbit: conjugate a parallel projection by a projectivity and it becomes a central one. So the distinction between Type A transfer (marks collected along a fibration) and Type B sighting (marks collected through a point) is not projective data at all; it is created by marking a hyperplane and asking whether the centre is incident with the mark. Duality is a projective operation, and it cannot preserve a distinction the projective group does not define. What it does to the mark is exact and instructive: affine space is (Pⁿ, H∞), and a duality carries the marked hyperplane to a marked point, δ(H∞) = O. The dual of the affine stage is the pinhole stage. The stages dualize; the plays do not, because dualizing also transposes the cast — ranges of point-marks become pencils of line-marks — so a sighting on the point-stage arrives as a pencil-play on the hyperplane-stage, never as a transfer between point-marks.

Worse, "duality" is not one map. The correlations of Pⁿ form a under the projective group; as δ ranges over them, δ(H∞) sweeps every point of the dual space, and the A/B classification on the dual side changes with the choice. There is no canonical answer to where infinity goes unless something anchors it, and a canonical duality is exactly a marked — a marked conic, a quadric in space — whose pole of the plane at infinity is its centre. The drawing board is straightedge-only and draws no conics. The missing duality anchor is the compass: the same instrument-poverty that makes even spacing non-constructible makes duality gauge-dependent. Give the board one drawn circle and it acquires a canonical centre, and the dual split snaps into place. That is where the mirror circle of Chapter 3 re-enters, now as the anchor of the whole engine.

5.2 · Stack and spine

In P³ the dual of a graduated of planes is a graduated book of planes whose is a line — a slit. The pencil of planes through the u-slit maps perspectivally onto the range of their trace lines on the picture plane, and a perspectivity of a pencil onto a range is a projective parametrization; the shipped cross-slit coordinate X₀/(1 − λru) is a Möbius function of λ, hence is a projective parameter of that pencil. So "cross-slit image coordinates are the two " is symbolic algebra, not a numerical question, and is theorem.

The naive identification fails at the next step, and the failure is the chapter's most useful fact. In the assembly, glide's two channels graduate by μu = λru and μv = λrv, both functions of depth alone; their level sets are the same family of parallel planes, calibrated twice — one axis, two scales. Dualize that and you get one spine carrying two graduations: coincident slits, the degenerate catalog, not the skew-slit congruence. So "the glide apparatus of an assembly dualizes to the cross-slit apparatus of the same assembly" is false as stated. What survives is a lemma and a sharpened conjecture. The lemma: two lines of P³ meet iff their duals meet, so the class of cross-slit congruences is self-dual — the dual of the lines meeting two slits is the lines meeting the two dual slits. The conjecture, which the check then tested: transpose = duality not within one assembly but as a conjugation across dual assemblies, cross-slit on K ≟ δ ∘ (glide on δK) ∘ δ, up to one fixed element of S₃ — the candidate being the seam relation ρτ = ω, which is exactly the algebraic gap between the two shipped gauges — with the fusion locus predicted to be the self-dual configurations.

5.3 · The six-step check, and the mirror

The protocol was fixed before the numbers were run: derive the anchor family from the rig's own furniture (slits mutually polar; picture plane and station exchanged), implement δ on Plücker coordinates, compare gauge scalars rather than pictures, identify the linking map against the six anharmonic elements, run on and off the seam, and state the falsifiers. The anchor family has three free parameters and one distinguished member, the sphere of radius √(Z₁Z₂), whose poles pair the picture plane with the depth point at infinity and the plane at infinity with the principal point. Under it the polar of every sampled congruence line is a congruence line, residual zero. The transport had to be read through a section, because the polar plane of a point on a line contains the whole dual line — the first machinery's bug, caught and reported — and the canonical section is the depth axis, the common perpendicular of the slits: there z† = Z₁Z₂/z, line-independent, fixed at the two points where the sphere crosses the axis.

The element identified itself. In the geometric-mean calibration ν = z/√(Z₁Z₂) the transport is 1/ν to 10⁻¹⁶ on and off the seam; in a single-channel calibration off the seam no element fits, correctly; in the cross-channel calibrations νu → νv† and νv → νu† are both exactly 1/ν. So δ acts on calibrated depth as (swap) ∘ ρ, and since ρτ = ω, conjugating the glide reading (τ per channel) by δ gives the cross-slit reading (ω per channel) up to ρ — the candidate, confirmed. The falsifier fired where it should: the swap-variant anchor, with a finite pole for the picture plane, fits no element in any calibration. Anharmonicity selects the geometric-mean sphere. The "marked conic" of §5.1 now has a name: the mirror-sphere R² = Z₁Z₂, VM·VI = VP² at anchor level.

On the seam the picture-trace constants of the transport diverge — structurally, not numerically. Off the seam δ maps the trace of a congruence line by a per-channel inversion, two mirror circles with radii in ratio −Z₁/Z₂; on the seam it sends the pinhole pencil to the line field of the slit plane, rays through the eye exchanged with lines in a plane. Collection exchanges with evaluation. The seam is where trace-level duality stops being an inversion and becomes the vector–covector swap itself — the corrected slogan of §5.1, now drawn: duality swaps collection and evaluation across the point/line divide, never within one cast.

5.4 · The station point, opened

The picture of all this is the station point pulled apart, and there are two ways to pull. Sheet 37 pulls it into two slits at two depths on the same axis of sight — the dial of Chapter 3 as a drawing, Zv = Zp − D/2, Zh = Zp + D/2, identically the pinhole at D = 0. Sheet 38 pulls it into two perpendicular armature axes, a vanishing axis that meters depth and a directing axis that assigns direction, the 1984 construction (pp. 88–90), with intercepts coupled by 1/k + 1/c = 1 so that the image of infinity opens from a point into a line. Both spend perspective's depth fidelity to buy breadth, and they spend it differently: the two slits are the camera's way, the two axes the draftsman's. The same scenes run on both sheets, diverging live from the common pinhole, and the divergence is the duality of this chapter seen from the drafting board.
